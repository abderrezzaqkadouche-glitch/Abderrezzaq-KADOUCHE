Identifying Clinical Risk Factors for the Early Detection of Diabetes

Abderrezzaq KADOUCHE

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a major public health challenge characterized by chronic hyperglycemia. Because metabolic abnormalities often develop silently over years, many patients remain undiagnosed until irreversible microvascular or macrovascular complications emerge. Identifying these predictors is therefore essential to improve early detection.

Objective

The primary objective of this study is to identify the specific clinical and physiological factors that predict the onset of diabetes.

Data source

This research utilizes a synthetic database to replicate the statistical properties of the historical medical study conducted by Smith, Everhart, Dickson, Knowler, and Johannes (1988). The original cohort targeted high-risk individuals, specifically female patients of Pima Native American heritage aged 21 and older, monitored by the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK).

Variable Specifications

This dataset contains 9 variables, including 8 clinical predictors and 1 binary outcome variable:

  • pregnant: Number of times pregnant. Counts the patient’s total number of pregnancies.
  • glucose: Plasma glucose concentration. Measured during a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test.
  • pressure: Diastolic blood pressure. Measured in millimeters of mercury (mm Hg).
  • triceps: Triceps skin fold thickness. Measured in millimeters (mm) to estimate subcutaneous fat.
  • insulin: 2-hour serum insulin. Measured in milli-international units per liter (mu U/ml).
  • mass: Body mass index (BMI). Calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (\(kg/m^2\)).
  • pedigree: Diabetes pedigree function. A score that represents the genetic risk based on family history.
  • age: Age. The patient’s age in years.
  • diabetes: Binary indicator representing the test result for diabetes (positive / negative).

Missing data

An analysis of the missingness patterns revealed a critical methodology challenge, particularly regarding insulin and triceps which present missingness rates of 52.73% and 32.68% respectively. In clinical settings, collecting 2-hour serum insulin levels and measuring triceps skinfold thickness require specific diagnostic tools and specialized procedures, making them frequently missing from standard patient charts. Conversely, other health indicators such as glucose, pressure, and mass show very low missingness rates (< 5%). Conducting a strict complete-case analysis on all nine variables would cause the sample size to plummet from 768 to 361 observations, which represents a data loss of 53%, sacrificing over half of the data and severely compromising the model’s statistical power.

Missing Data Management

A complete-case analysis was applied. This approach filters the dataset to retain only the observations with fully documented records across all 9 variables, including insulin and triceps. While this strategy significantly reduces the final sample size to 361 observations due to the high missingness rates in insulin and skinfold measurements, it allows for a comprehensive model that evaluates the simultaneous impact of every studied variable.

Statistical Modeling and Diagnostics

To evaluate the combined influence of the studied physiological indicators on the outcome, a multiple logistic regression model was implemented.

# Ajustement du modèle : régression logistique multiple

mod <- glm(diabetes ~ ., data = complete_data, family = binomial)

Prior to interpreting the model’s coefficients, the underlying statistical assumptions were rigorously audited to ensure the validity, stability, and reliability of our inferences. All diagnostic checks were successfully satisfied: First, the sample size sufficiency was verified using the Events Per Variable (EPV) rule; The standard threshold of at least 10 events per explanatory variable was fully validated. Furthermore, the assumption of log-linearity for the continuous predictors was assessed and satisfied, confirming a linear relationship with the log-odds of the outcome. Finally, all other diagnostic checks were successfully met: the Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) confirmed the complete absence of multicollinearity, Cook’s distance values remained safely under critical thresholds, indicating no highly influential individual profiles.

Results

Statistically Significant Predictors

  • Plasma Glucose Concentration (glucose): This variable was found to be the most highly significant predictor in the model (β = 0.0356, p < 0.001). Transforming this coefficient into an Odds Ratio yields OR = 1.04 (\(95\%\text{ CI: }[1.03, 1.05]\)). This means that for each additional unit (mg/dL) of plasma glucose, the odds of a positive diabetes diagnosis increase by exactly 4.0%, keeping all other variables constant.
  • Diabetes Pedigree Function (pedigree): The genetic family history score also displays a statistically significant positive effect on the outcome. It carries a powerful Odds Ratio of OR = 2.46 (\(95\%\text{ CI: }[1.24, 5.03]\)). This indicates that a one-unit increase in the pedigree function score more than doubles the odds of diabetes, multiplying them by 2.46.

Non-Significant Predictors

The remaining factors did not achieve statistical significance at the 5% level, meaning their independent associations with a positive diabetes status could not be confirmed in this multivariable context:

  • Body Mass Index (mass): Although displaying a positive trend, the effect was not significant (β = 0.0396, OR = 1.04, p = 0.1138).

  • 2-Hour Serum Insulin (insulin): The effect was negligible and non-significant (β = 0.0018, OR = 1.00, p = 0.1543).

  • Triceps Skin Fold Thickness (triceps): Showed no significant relationship (β = 0.0212, OR = 1.02, p = 0.2418).

  • Age (age): Did not reach the significance threshold (β = 0.0159, OR = 1.02, p = 0.3271).

  • Diastolic Blood Pressure (pressure): Showed non-significant coefficient (β = 0.0080, OR = 1.01, p = 0.5290).

  • Number of Pregnancies (pregnant): Displayed the highest p-value among all predictors (β = 0.0197, OR = 1.02, p = 0.7143).

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of our complete-case findings and mitigate the loss of statistical power caused by the exclusion of 53% of the sample, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using multiple imputation. The missing data mechanism was assumed to be Missing At Random (MAR).

Comparison of Results (Complete-Case vs. Multiple Imputation): The comparison between the complete-case analysis (CC) and the multiple imputation (MI) yields crucial insights:

Consistency of Primary Predictors:

The two main findings of the study remain perfectly robust. Plasma glucose stays highly significant in both models, with an almost identical Odds Ratio (OR = 1.04 CC vs. OR = 1.03 MI, p < 0.001). Similarly, the Diabetes Pedigree Function remains a strong, independent predictor (OR = 2.46 CC vs. OR = 1.76 MI, p = 0.023), confirming that genetic predisposition and glucose tolerance are the paramount risk factors regardless of the missing data strategy.

Changes in Non-Significant Predictors:

The multiple imputation analysis reveals some interesting shifts in variables that were borderline or non-significant in the complete-case model. Most notably, mass (BMI) transitions from a non-significant trend in the complete-case analysis (OR = 1.04, p = 0.113) to statistical significance in the MI model (OR = 1.046, p = 0.011). A similar shift is observed for the number of pregnancies pregnant, which becomes significant in the MI model (OR = 1.083, p = 0.019) compared to the CC model (p = 0.714).

library(mice)

  data,

  maxit  = 10,

  imp,

Conclusion

The multiple imputation sensitivity analysis validates the core conclusions of the study while providing a more comprehensive and statistically powerful view of secondary risk factors. It confirms that clinical screening should prioritize glucose tolerance and genetic history, while acknowledging that BMI and pregnancy history play significant secondary roles in early detection.
