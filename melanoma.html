Prognostic Factors of Melanoma-Specific Mortality: A Retrospective Cohort

Competing Risk Analysis to Account for Non-Melanoma Deaths

Abderrezzaq KADOUCHE

Introduction

Malignant cutaneous melanoma is one of the most aggressive forms of skin cancer. Evaluating its long-term survival outcomes is critical for developing prognostic profiles and public health strategies. While randomized clinical trials offer a controlled environment to study efficacy, they often apply strict exclusion criteria that mask the complexities of real-world populations. This study is contextualized within the field of Real-World Evidence (RWE), analyzing historical clinical data from patients who underwent definitive surgical treatment. In this non-randomized, real-world setting, patients are older, present multiple comorbidities, and face a dual threat: the progression of their primary cancer or death from unrelated medical events. Understanding the interplay between tumor characteristics at the time of surgery and long-term mortality—while mathematically separating cancer virulence from background population mortality—provides the necessary epidemiological context to optimize patient care and survival tracking.

Objective

• Primary Objective: To estimate the true absolute risk of melanoma-specific mortality over time by implementing a robust competing risks framework.

• Secondary Objective: To quantify the impact of key clinical factors —specifically tumour thickness and the presence of histological ulceration— on disease-specific mortality.

Study Design

This is a retrospective, observational cohort study based on historical, longitudinal medical records. Because the investigator did not assign the intervention (surgical removal of the tumor was standard clinical practice for all diagnosed individuals), the study relies on the passive observation of patient trajectories over a multi-year follow-up period.

Data Source

The clinical observations analyzed in this research originate from the seminal study conducted by P. K. Andersen, O. Borgan, R. D. Gill, and N. Keiding (1993), published in their foundational work Statistical Models based on Counting Processes. This historical dataset serves as the core empirical basis for our survival and competing risks assessments.

• Study Population: The baseline sample comprises 205 patients diagnosed with malignant cutaneous melanoma who underwent complete surgical resection at the Department of Plastic Surgery, University Hospital of Odense, Denmark. The recruitment and surgical interventions took place between 1962 and 1977.

• Follow-up Period: Patients were followed prospectively from the exact date of their surgical procedure until an administrative censoring date at the end of 1977.

Variable Specifications

• time : Survival time measured in days from the exact date of the surgical operation to the date of death or last follow-up.

• status : (1 = Melanoma Death, 2 = Alive, 3 = Other Death) : Multi-state categorical endpoint representing the mutually exclusive final events, where death from other causes acts as the competing risk to the primary event of interest.

• thickness : Tumour thickness measured in millimeters (mm), representing the Breslow depth of penetration of the primary melanoma.

• ulcer : (1 = Presence, 0 = Absence): Histological binary indicator showing the presence or absence of ulceration on the surface of the primary tumour.

• age : Patient’s age in years at the time of the surgical procedure.

• sex : 1 = Male, 0 = Female.

• year : Calendar year in which the surgical operation was performed, ranging from 1962 to 1977.

Cumulative Incidence of Melanoma-Specific Death

Cumulative incidence functions for melanoma-specific death were estimated, treating death from other causes as a competing risk. The presence of ulceration was associated with a markedly higher cumulative incidence of melanoma death compared with non-ulcerated tumors (Gray’s test, p < 0.001), with no significant association for death from other causes (p = 0.69). Similarly, patients with a high tumor thickness (> 1.94 mm) had a significantly higher cumulative incidence of melanoma death than those with a low thickness (≤ 1.94 mm; Gray’s test, p < 0.001), while no significant association was found for death from other causes (p = 0.99). Male sex was also associated with a significantly higher cumulative incidence of melanoma death compared with female sex (Gray’s test, p = 0.016), with no significant association for death from other causes (p = 0.36). These findings confirm the prognostic value of ulceration, tumor thickness, and sex specifically for melanoma-specific mortality, while properly accounting for the competing risk of death from other causes.

Statistical Modeling and Diagnostics

To account for the presence of a competing event (death from other causes), a Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model was fitted for the event of interest (melanoma-specific death). The status variable was recoded as a competing risk outcome with three ordered levels: censoring, melanoma death (event of interest), and death from other causes (competing event). The model included ulceration, tumor thickness, age, and sex as covariates. The regression coefficients from the Fine and Gray model were reported as subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), allowing interpretation of the effect of each covariate on the cumulative incidence of melanoma-specific death while appropriately accounting for the competing risk of death from other causes.

The proportional subdistribution hazards assumption of the Fine and Gray model was assessed using the Schoenfeld residual test. The global test indicated a violation of the assumption (p = 0.038). At the individual covariate level, significant time-varying effects were observed for ulceration (p = 0.038) and tumor thickness (p = 0.010), as illustrated by the non-horizontal smoothed trends of the scaled Schoenfeld residuals over time. No significant violations were found for age (p = 0.459) or sex (p = 0.215). These results suggest that the effects of ulceration and tumor thickness on the cumulative incidence of melanoma-specific death may not be constant over time, indicating a need for cautious interpretation of the corresponding subdistribution hazard ratios. However, when time-dependent interaction terms were introduced into the model, none were statistically significant (ulcer × log(time): p = 0.3; thickness × log(time): p = 0.8). Given these findings, the model without interactions was retained for the main analysis, and the subdistribution hazard ratios should be interpreted with caution in light of a potential time-varying effect.

Results

The presence of ulceration was significantly associated with an increased subdistribution hazard of melanoma death (sHR = 3.09; 95% CI: 1.71–5.60; p < 0.001).

Similarly, higher tumor thickness was associated with a significantly increased hazard (sHR = 1.09 ; 95% CI: 1.01–1.18; p = 0.019).

In contrast, age (sHR = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.99–1.02; p = 0.5) and male sex (sHR = 1.50; 95% CI: 0.87–2.57; p = 0.14) were not significantly associated with melanoma-specific death.

Conclusion

In this cohort of patients with malignant melanoma, ulceration and tumor thickness were independent prognostic factors for melanoma-specific death, even after accounting for the competing risk of death from other causes.

These findings underscore the importance of these two clinical parameters in the initial risk stratification and management of melanoma. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up may be needed to clarify the prognostic role of sex and age in this setting.
